# What Will Power the Grid in 2035? The Race Is Wide Open

If you could jump into a time machine and peek at the electrical grid in 2035, what would you see? Will we finally be running on limitless clean fusion energy? Will sleek new nuclear fission plants dot the countryside? Or will natural gas—still—be the backbone of our power supply, now with carbon capture to clean up its act? The truth is, nobody knows for sure. And that’s what makes this moment so fascinating: the race to power the grid in the early 2030s is genuinely wide open, with three very different technologies neck-and-neck.

For decades, we’ve been promised a clean energy revolution. Renewables like wind and solar have made huge strides, but they still face the intermittency problem—the sun doesn’t always shine, and the wind doesn’t always blow. To keep the lights on 24/7, we need reliable, dispatchable power. That’s where fusion, fission, and even modernized natural gas come in. And right now, all three are in the running, each with its own strengths, challenges, and timelines. Let’s break down the contenders.

## 🌟 Fusion: The Dream That’s (Maybe) Finally Coming True

Nuclear fusion—the process that powers the sun—has been the holy grail of energy for over half a century. The promise is almost too good to be true: abundant fuel (just deuterium from seawater and lithium for tritium), no long-lived radioactive waste, and inherent safety (no meltdown risk). But the engineering challenges have proven immense: sustaining a plasma at 100 million degrees, handling the intense neutron bombardment, and achieving net energy gain.

The good news? We’re closer than ever. The international ITER project in France is on schedule for first plasma in the mid-2030s, aiming to demonstrate Q≥10 (ten times more fusion energy than heating power). Meanwhile, a new wave of private companies—Commonwealth Fusion Systems, TAE Technologies, Helion—are pursuing more compact, potentially cheaper designs, with aggressive timelines targeting demonstration plants by the early 2030s and commercial power by the 2040s.

**The 2035 outlook:** Don’t expect fusion to be a major grid contributor yet. But if any demo plant comes online and proves the technology, it could start shaping investment and policy by the late 2030s. Fusion is the long-term hope, but 2035 is probably still a bit early for widespread deployment.

## ⚛️ Nuclear Fission: The Steady Workhorse Gets a Makeover

While fusion grabs headlines, fission—the technology that already provides about 10% of global electricity—is quietly having a renaissance. The new generation isn’t your grandfather’s huge, expensive, controversial reactors. We’re talking about **Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)** and **Generation IV designs** that promise to be safer, cheaper, and faster to build.

SMRs can be factory-built and shipped to sites, reducing construction time and cost. Companies like NuScale (U.S.), Rolls-Royce (U.K.), and China National Nuclear Corp are advancing designs that could start coming online in the 2027–2030 window. Some SMRs are even designed to replace coal plant sites, leveraging existing grid connections.

Generation IV reactors—molten salt, sodium-cooled fast reactors, high-temperature gas-cooled—offer higher efficiency, the ability to consume nuclear waste as fuel, and potentially even produce hydrogen. They’re further out (2035+ for commercial), but the research is promising.

**The 2035 outlook:** Fission is the most likely candidate to significantly increase its share by 2035, especially if regulatory frameworks streamline and public acceptance improves. SMRs could add tens of gigawatts globally by then, providing stable, carbon-free baseload power.

## 🛢️ Natural Gas with Carbon Capture: The Pragmatic Bridge

Let’s not forget the fossil fuel elephant in the room. Natural gas already accounts for a large and growing slice of global power generation, especially in the U.S., Middle East, and parts of Asia. It’s flexible, relatively cheap, and the infrastructure is mature. The problem? Carbon emissions—and methane leaks from the supply chain.

Enter **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)**. The idea is simple: capture CO₂ from the power plant flue gas, compress it, and inject it deep underground. The technology exists; it’s just expensive and not yet deployed at massive scale. A few demonstration projects (like Petra Nova in Texas and the Boundary Dam in Canada) have shown it can work, but economics are tough without strong carbon pricing or subsidies.

What could change the equation? The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act provides a $50/ton tax credit for captured CO₂, dramatically improving project economics. If gas prices stay moderate and CCS costs fall to ~$30–40/ton, gas+CCS could become cost-competitive with renewables plus storage in some regions.

**The 2035 outlook:** In a world where climate pressure mounts but energy demand keeps rising, many countries may keep building efficient gas plants paired with CCS. Expect to see at least a few gigawatts of such plants operating by 2035, especially in regions with indigenous gas resources and limited renewable potential (e.g., parts of the U.S. Midwest, Eastern Europe, Japan).

## 🌬️ The Wild Card: Renewables + Storage Keep Improving

Okay, we said three contenders, but let’s be real: solar and wind are already winning the race in terms of new capacity additions. The real bottleneck is their intermittency, and that’s where battery storage—and other long-duration storage technologies—come in. The cost of lithium-ion batteries has fallen 90% in the last decade, and 4–8 hour storage systems are now economical in many markets.

But to fully replace firm power sources, we’ll need longer storage—think 10, 20, even 100 hours. That’s where emerging tech like flow batteries, compressed air, thermal storage, and green hydrogen could play a role. By 2035, we might see the first utility-scale demonstrations of these long-duration systems, which could finally allow renewables to dominate the grid without needing as much backup from gas or fission.

**The 2025–2035 window:** Renewables will likely remain the largest source of new generation, but their grid penetration will be capped without massive storage deployment or firm low-carbon backup (i.e., fission or gas+CCS). So the “race” isn’t about which single technology wins—it’s about finding the right mix.

## 🏁 The Bottom Line: It’s All About Mix and Match

If we had to place bets today, the most probable 2035 grid looks something like this:
- 40–50% renewables (wind/solar) plus growing storage
- 15–20% nuclear fission (mostly existing plus some SMRs)
- 10–15% natural gas (with a meaningful fraction equipped with CCS)
- <5% hydro and other sources
- Possibly 1–2% fusion (if demos are very successful)

In other words, **the grid of 2035 will be powered by a diverse portfolio**, not a single silver bullet. The exact mix will depend on:
- Policy decisions (carbon pricing, subsidies, permitting reforms)
- Technological breakthroughs (cost reductions in fusion, SMR, CCS, storage)
- Public acceptance (nuclear still faces NIMBYism in many places)
- Geopolitics (energy security concerns driving nuclear vs. gas choices)

The race is wide open because we’re in a period of disruption. Old assumptions are being challenged, new players are emerging, and the cost curves for clean technologies are still falling fast. The next decade will determine whether we double down on a few mature technologies or unleash a broader range of solutions. One thing’s for sure: watching this race will be electrifying.
