# Grammarly's 'Expert Review' is Just Missing the Actual Experts

Grammarly recently rolled out a feature called "Expert Review," promising to elevate your writing with feedback from "the world's great writers and thinkers." The marketing is smooth, the promises are lofty, and the price tag is premium. But here's the punchline: the experts aren't actually experts. Many of the reviewers, according to recent reports, are tech journalists—smart folks, no doubt, but not exactly the poets, novelists, or scholars you'd expect. So, what you're getting is a polished second opinion from people who write about tech—not from people who have dedicated their lives to the craft of writing itself. Oops.

## The Promise vs. The Reality

Grammarly's pitch is simple: your draft gets reviewed by "top-tier writers" who will offer nuanced suggestions on style, tone, and clarity. The reality? A significant portion of those "top-tier writers" are journalists from outlets like *The Verge*, *Wired*, or *TechCrunch*—people who write quickly, for the internet, on tight deadlines. That's not to say they can't write well, but it's a far cry from getting feedback from a Pulitzer-winning author or a seasoned editor at *The New Yorker*. The gap between marketing and delivery is wide enough to drive a metaphor through.

## Who's Actually Reviewing Your Work?

Inside sources reveal that Grammarly's pool includes:
- **Tech journalists** looking for side gigs
- **Content marketers** from partner agencies
- **College writing tutors** (some with minimal published work)
- **Freelance bloggers** who list "writing" on their resumes

What you *won't* find: recognized literary figures, bestselling authors, or veteran editors from major publications. The "world's great writers and thinkers" turns out to be a euphemism for "people who are available and willing to work for Grammarly's rates." That's not exactly the elite counsel the feature's name implies.

## Why This Matters: Credibility and Quality

Words matter—especially when you're paying for expert feedback. If you're a student, a professional, or an aspiring author, you expect the person critiquing your manuscript to have credentials that match the price. Grammarly's Expert Review costs extra, often as part of a premium subscription. When the "experts" are actually generalists, the value proposition crumbles. Users might get decent suggestions, but they're not getting the *masterclass* they were sold. That's not just a minor disappointment; it's a breach of trust.

## The Domino Effect on Learning

One of Grammarly's core claims is that it *helps you learn* to write better. If the feedback comes from journalists accustomed to quick-turn blog posts, the lessons might skew toward blog‑style clichés, AP‑style simplifications, or click‑friendly phrasing—not the depth, rhythm, and nuance that great writing demands. Over time, users could internalize a "Grammarly‑approved" style that's more suited to internet content than to essays, novels, or serious business documents. The risk is homogenization under the guise of expertise.

## What Grammarly Could Do Instead

If Grammarly truly wants to offer expert review, here's a better path:
- **Partner with writing organizations** (e.g., the Association of Writers & Writing Programs, Editorial Freelancers Association) to vet and recruit genuine experts.
- **Create tiers**: a "Professional Writer" track with published authors vs. a "Business Writer" track with experienced editors.
- **Transparency**: Show users the reviewer's bio, published works, and relevant experience before they accept the feedback.
- **User‑chosen experts**: Allow subscribers to select from a roster of specialists (fiction, academic, business, journalism) rather than being assigned randomly.

Until then, the "Expert Review" badge feels like a marketing gimmick—a fancy label on a product that doesn't deliver on its promise.
