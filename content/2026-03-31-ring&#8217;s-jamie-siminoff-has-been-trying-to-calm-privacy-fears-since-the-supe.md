# Ring's Jamie Siminoff has been trying to calm privacy fears since the Super Bowl, but his answers may not help

Jamie Siminoff, the founder of Ring, took the stage at the 2022 Super Bowl with a bold vision: smart doorbells protecting neighborhoods. But just a year later, privacy advocates, lawmakers, and everyday users are asking hard questions—and Siminoff's recent responses are doing little to ease concerns. The heart of the issue? **facial recognition**. Ring's doorbells can now not only record video but potentially identify faces, raising the specter of mass surveillance in our own front yards. Siminoff says he's listening, but his explanations feel more like deflection than reassurance.

## The Facial Recognition Question Is Where Things Get Tangled

Ring's products have evolved from simple video doorbells to full **home security ecosystems** with cameras, alarms, and—critically—facial recognition capabilities. While Ring claims the feature is optional and only identifies "known faces" you've tagged, the implications are massive:

- **Warrantless surveillance**: Neighbors, delivery people, strangers—all captured and potentially stored in Ring's cloud.
- **Data sharing with law enforcement**: Ring has partnerships with thousands of police departments, raising questions about how facial data could be used without a warrant.
- **Bias and accuracy**: Facial recognition technology is notoriously error-prone, especially for people of color and women—misidentification could have serious consequences.

When asked whether Ring's facial recognition could enable real-time tracking across neighborhoods, Siminoff has been evasive, focusing on "opt-in" and "user control" without directly addressing the broader societal risks.

## Siminoff's Answers Sound Good but Lack Substance

In interviews and congressional hearings, Siminoff has repeated a few key lines:

> "We believe privacy is a fundamental human right."

> "Facial recognition is an opt-in feature, and users control their data."

> "We don't sell data to advertisers."

These sound like privacy-friendly positions—until you dig deeper:

- **Opt-in doesn't mean safe**: Even if you don't use facial recognition, your Ring camera could still capture neighbors, passersby, and visitors, storing their biometric data without consent.
- **"Control" is limited**: Users can delete their own data, but Ring's data retention policies and third-party sharing with law enforcement remain murky.
- **No-selling ≠ no-sharing**: Ring's law enforcement partnerships allow police to request video footage directly from users, often without a warrant. Facial recognition could make those requests more targeted and invasive.

Siminoff's answers focus on **user-level privacy** while glossing over **community-level harms**. Your decision to use facial recognition doesn't prevent your neighbor's Ring from identifying you walking down the street.

## The "Neighborhood Watch" Framing Is a Double-Edged Sword

Ring markets itself as a modern neighborhood watch—empowering communities to protect themselves. But when that watch includes **facial recognition**, it becomes something else entirely:

- **Normalization of surveillance**: Cameras become invisible, expected, even demanded. What price security when every movement is recorded?
- **Privatized policing**: Ring's partnerships effectively give police a network of private surveillance cameras, bypassing public debate and oversight.
- **Chilling effects**: People may alter their behavior—avoiding certain homes, skipping protests, hesitating to visit friends—knowing they could be identified.

Siminoff touts the safety benefits, but he hasn't convincingly addressed how Ring's technology could be misused by authoritarian-leaning local governments or bad actors who gain access to the data.

## Why His Reassurances May Be Too Late

Privacy fears around Ring aren't new—they've simmered for years. But recent events have brought them to a boil:

- **Congressional investigations** into Ring's data practices and law enforcement partnerships.
- **Reports of data breaches** exposing user videos.
- **Growing public awareness** of facial recognition's risks, especially for marginalized communities.

Siminoff's responses have consistently been **reactive, not proactive**. He addresses concerns only after they make headlines, and his answers often repeat the same talking points without substantive policy changes. Meanwhile, competitors like Arlo and Google Nest are starting to distance themselves from facial recognition or implement stricter controls.

## The Bottom Line: Trust Can't Be Rebuilt with Words

Jamie Siminoff may genuinely believe in Ring's mission, but his PR-friendly answers don't match the reality of Ring's technology and partnerships. Facial recognition in residential doorbells isn't just a "feature"—it's a fundamental shift in the balance between security and privacy. Until Ring:

- **Publishes transparent audit reports** on law enforcement requests and facial recognition usage
- **Implements strong default restrictions** (e.g., no facial recognition without explicit, granular consent from all parties)
- **Allows truly local-only storage** without mandatory cloud uploads
- **Advocates for public regulations** that limit biometric surveillance in residential areas

...his assurances will ring hollow. The Super Bowl ad promised safety, but the fine print may be selling us a surveillance state, one doorbell at a time.

---

Ring's story is a cautionary tale: when technology outpaces ethics, even the most well-intentioned founder can't talk his way out of a crisis. Privacy isn't just a feature to toggle—it's a right that needs protecting by design.
