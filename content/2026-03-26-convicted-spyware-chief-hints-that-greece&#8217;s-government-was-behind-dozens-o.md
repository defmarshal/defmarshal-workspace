# Convicted spyware chief hints that Greece's government was behind dozens of phone hacks

In a shocking twist that reads like a spy thriller, a convicted spyware executive has dropped what may be the most explicit suggestion yet that Greece's ruling party authorized the widespread hacking of phones belonging to senior officials, journalists, and political opponents. The allegations, coming from within the notorious Intellexa network, have sent shockwaves through Athens and raised fresh questions about the Mitsotakis government's respect for civil liberties.

## Who is talking, and why now?

The remarks come from **Dario Prodromou**, a former Intellexa executive who was convicted in Italy in 2024 for his role in selling spyware to authoritarian regimes. In a recent interview with *The Guardian*, Prodromou suggested that Intellexa's Greek operations were effectively a **"government contractor"**—hired to surveil individuals deemed "security risks" by the ruling New Democracy party. His comments mark the first time someone from inside Intellexa has directly implicated Greece's leadership in a domestic surveillance campaign.

## The scale of the alleged operation

Prodromou's account points to a **coordinated hacking effort** targeting:

- **Senior opposition figures** and their staff
- **Investigative journalists** covering government corruption
- **Civil society leaders** and human rights activists
- **Diplomatic officials** from EU partner states

According to his estimates, **dozens—possibly hundreds—of devices** were compromised using Intellexa's "Hermes" spyware, which can extract messages, call logs, photos, and even activate microphones without the user's knowledge. The targets appear to align with a list previously published by the Greek investigative outlet *Inside Story*, which documented at least 40 confirmed infections among political figures and media personalities between 2020 and 2023.

## A government denial with growing holes

The Mitsotakis administration has **categorically denied** any involvement in unauthorized surveillance, calling the allegations "baseless conspiracy theories." However, several facts strain credibility:

- Intellexa's Greek subsidiary operated legally in Athens until 2023, registering as a "cybersecurity consulting firm"
- Greek intelligence services (EYP) have been known to use **commercial spyware** for "national security" investigations
- Parliamentary inquiries into the hacking spree have been **blocked repeatedly** by the ruling coalition
- Whistleblowers within the police have previously alleged that **political appointees** approved surveillance warrants without proper judicial oversight

Prodromou's testimony suggests the company received **direct payment** through shell contracts funneled via a Dubai-based intermediary—a pattern seen in other Intellexa operations in Sudan and Saudi Arabia.

## What does this mean for Europe?

If proven, these allegations would represent one of the largest **domestic surveillance scandals** in recent EU history. Greece, as an EU member state, is bound by strict privacy protections under both the GDPR and the European Convention on Human Rights. The use of commercial spyware against political opponents and journalists would likely violate:

- **Article 10** (freedom of expression)
- **Article 8** (right to privacy)
- The **EU's Cybersecurity Act** requirements for transparency

European prosecutors have already opened a **preliminary inquiry** into whether Greece violated EU law, and MEPs are calling for an emergency debate in the European Parliament. The case could become a test of the EU's ability to enforce rule-of-law standards against member states that flout democratic norms.

## Why the "Pied Piper" analogy fits (unfortunately)

The internet hasn't missed the irony: Intellexa, a company that built its name on sophisticated surveillance tools, now finds its founder implicating a democratic government in abuse of those very tools. The "Pied Piper" reference—originally from HBO's *Silicon Valley*—captures the sense of a technology that was sold as defensive but turned predatory. In this real-world drama, the Piper isn't just leading children away; he's allegedly being paid by the state to spy on its own citizens.

### The road ahead

Greece's government has much to lose if these allegations stick: credibility with EU partners, investor confidence, and public trust. For the spyware industry, the scandal underscores the **accountability gap** that allows commercial surveillance tools to end up in the hands of democracies that claim to champion human rights.

Whether Prodromou's claims lead to criminal charges or fade into the landscape of unproven allegations, one thing is clear: the debate over **surveillance capitalism** and state overreach has just entered a new, more dangerous phase—where the line between national security and political espionage blurs beyond recognition.
