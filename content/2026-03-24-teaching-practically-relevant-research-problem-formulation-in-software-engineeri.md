# Teaching Practically Relevant Research Problem Formulation in Software Engineering with Lean Research Inception

Ever felt that pang of frustration when you pour your heart into a software engineering research project, only to realize—after months of work—that the problem you solved wasn't the one that actually matters to practitioners? You're not alone. The gap between academic research and industrial relevance has been a persistent chasm in SE. But what if we could teach students to *start* with the right problem—right from the very beginning? Enter **Lean Research Inception (LRI)**, a refreshingly practical framework that's transforming how we teach research problem formulation.

## The Problem: Academia vs. Industry Misalignment

Traditional SE research training often emphasizes technical depth over contextual relevance. Students learn to craft novel methodologies and conduct rigorous experiments, but they rarely practice the crucial first step: **defining a problem that matters**. The result? Papers that get published in academic circles but gather dust in industry trenches. LRI flips this script by making industry engagement a *starting point*, not an afterthought.

## What is Lean Research Inception?

LRI adapts the "inception" mindset from lean startup culture—focus on validated learning, rapid feedback, and customer (here, industry stakeholder) collaboration. In a research context, it's a structured, time-boxed process where students:

- **Conduct lightweight stakeholder interviews** with actual practitioners (not just imaginary "industry partners")
- **Define a problem hypothesis** in one sentence: "We believe [practitioners] struggle with [specific pain] because [assumed cause], leading to [impact]."
- **Create a minimal research plan** that could yield a usable artifact within weeks, not years.
- **Validate or invalidate** their hypothesis by the end of the course—success is learning, not necessarily publishing.

The key is *lean*: no lengthy literature reviews before talking to humans, no over-engineered proposals. Just a clear, falsifiable problem statement and a plan to test it quickly.

## Key Teaching Techniques

### 1. "Problem Speed Dating"
Students rotate through 10-minute virtual sessions with industry volunteers (developers, tech leads, QA managers). They ask only: "What's the most annoying part of your current workflow?" Notes are taken, patterns emerge, and students pick one concrete pain point to own. This forces them to listen first, conceptualize later.

### 2. The One-Page Canvas
Inspired by the business model canvas, students fill a single page with:
- Problem statement (in user's words)
- Assumed cause
- Proposed artifact (tool, guideline, metric)
- Success criteria (observable improvement)
- Risks (why it might not matter)

This canvas becomes their north star—everything else supports or challenges it.

### 3. Rapid Prototyping as Validation
Before writing any code, students build a *paper prototype*: sketches, storyboards, or even a 5-minute video demonstrating how a practitioner would use their proposed solution. They show this to stakeholders and iterate. If the stakeholder yawns, the problem likely isn't juicy enough.

### 4. "Kill Criteria" in Advance
Students must define, upfront, what would make them *abandon* their chosen problem. E.g., "If 3 out of 5 stakeholders say they already have a workaround, we pivot." This cultivates intellectual humility and prevents sunk-cost fallacy.

## Why This Works

- **Authentic motivation**: Students feel accountable to real people, not just a grade.
- **Early failure is cheap**: It's better to discover a problem is trivial in two weeks than two years.
- **Skills transfer**: The LRI process itself is a portable skill—students can apply it to any future research or even product development.
- **Bidirectional learning**: Industry partners gain fresh perspectives; students gain context.

## The Ripple Effect

When we introduced LRI into our graduate SE seminar, something magical happened: student projects started *ending* with actionable artifacts—templates, checklists, lightweight tools—that local companies actually adopted. More importantly, students reported a mindset shift: they stopped looking for "cool research gaps" and started hunting for "pain worth solving."

---

### Conclusion

Lean Research Inception isn't just a teaching method; it's an attitude. It reminds us that research relevance is not a bolt-on extra—it's the foundation. By equipping students with a structured, lean approach to problem formulation, we're not just producing better papers; we're producing researchers who ask better questions from the start. And in software engineering, as in life, the right question is worth more than a thousand answers.

*Teaching research? Start with the problem. Always.*
