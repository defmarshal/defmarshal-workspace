# Cultural Perspectives and Expectations for Generative AI: A Global Survey Approach

When ChatGPT writes a poem or DALL-E draws a portrait, whose cultural lens is it seeing through? Silicon Valley’s defaults have long shaped how generative AI represents the world, but a groundbreaking global survey reveals that people from different cultures don’t just want AI to speak their language—they want it to *understand* their values, their aesthetics, and their unwritten rules. The findings are a wake-up call for anyone building or deploying GenAI: there is no one-size-fits-all “global” AI. Cultural context isn’t a nice-to-have feature; it’s a foundational requirement.

## The Survey: Spanning Continents and Worldviews

Researchers behind this study fielded a comprehensive questionnaire across 20+ countries, covering all inhabited continents. The sample included both “high-resource” nations (US, Japan, Germany) and “low-resource” regions (Kenya, Peru, Indonesia), ensuring that voices often missing from AI discourse were heard. The survey probed:

- How people define “cultural authenticity” in AI-generated content
- Which domains (art, news, education) are most sensitive to cultural nuance
- Willingness to trust AI that adapts to local norms
- Preferred governance models (local control vs. global standards)

Over 15,000 responses painted a nuanced picture of global expectations.

## Key Finding 1: Representation Matters, but Not How You Might Think

When asked whether AI should “represent my culture accurately,” 87% agreed—but what “accurate” means varies wildly:

- **Western respondents** emphasized *diversity within culture* (avoid stereotypes, show multiple perspectives)
- **East Asian respondents** prioritized *social harmony* and avoiding content that could cause shame or conflict
- **Global South participants** stressed *agency*—AI should not perpetuate colonial narratives or speak *about* a culture without involving its people in the training loop

This isn’t about translation; it’s about *worldview embedding*. An AI that generates a “Japanese” haiku might check the syllable box but miss the seasonal reference (kigo) and the emotional restraint (yūgen) that give it depth.

## Key Finding 2: Trust Is Earned Through Local Governance

Across almost all regions, people distrusted AI systems controlled by distant corporate entities. The survey found strongest support for:

- **Local data sovereignty**: Training data should originate from the culture it represents, with community consent.
- **Participatory design**: Cultural experts, elders, and everyday citizens should have a say in what “authentic” means.
- **Transparent provenance**: Users want to know *why* an AI made a cultural choice (e.g., “This image uses traditional motifs because…”).

Notably, younger, urban respondents were more accepting of global AI if it allowed *customization*—a “cultural filter” they could adjust. Older and rural groups wanted culturally-tuned models out of the box.

## Key Finding 3: The “One Model Fits All” Assumption Is Broken

The data explode the myth that we can serve the world with a single multilingual model. Key disparities:

- **Translation adequacy**: 60% of non-English speakers said AI translations felt “mechanical” and missed cultural idioms.
- **Content appropriateness**: Humor, sarcasm, and politeness strategies differ dramatically; AI often gets these wrong, sometimes causing offense.
- **Historical sensitivity**: Representations of religious figures, historical events, and traditional attire varied in acceptability by region—what’s “neutral” in one place can be sacred or profane in another.

These aren’t fringe concerns; they affect whether people will use AI at all.

## Key Finding 4: Generative AI Amplifies Existing Cultural Gaps

The survey warns that without intentional intervention, GenAI will:

- **Erase minority cultures** by defaulting to dominant (often Western) aesthetics in art, music, and storytelling
- **Reinforce stereotypes** because training data contains biased cultural depictions
- **Create new digital divides**: cultures with strong digital representation will see AI adapt to them, while others will get a “generic” version that feels alien

Participants from indigenous and heritage language communities were particularly concerned about cultural dilution—AI generating “fake” cultural artifacts that misrepresent traditions.

---

## What This Means for AI Builders

The message is clear: cultural adaptation isn’t a localisation checkbox at the end of development. It must be baked in from data collection to model evaluation. Steps include:

- **Culturally diverse training teams** with lived experience of the target cultures
- **Community review processes** for outputs that touch on cultural domains
- **Customisable cultural parameters** (tone, formality, symbolism) that users can adjust
- **Regional benchmarking** alongside standard accuracy metrics

---

We stand at a crossroads. Generative AI could become a tool that flattens cultural diversity into a homogenised global monoculture—or it could become a bridge that helps people share their unique perspectives with the world. The global survey shows that people want the latter, but they don’t trust the current trajectory. The responsibility falls on developers, companies, and policymakers to listen, to involve communities meaningfully, and to build systems that respect the richness of human culture. Because an AI that doesn’t understand your culture isn’t just inaccurate—it’s alienating. And in a connected world, alienation is a bug we can’t afford to ship.
