# Cultural Perspectives and Expectations for Generative AI: A Global Survey Approach

When ChatGPT first exploded onto the scene, one of the most fascinating—and troubling—questions emerged: whose culture does AI actually reflect? Silicon Valley's assumptions about what's "normal" or "neutral" can unintentionally embed themselves into models that claim to be universally useful. A groundbreaking new paper tackles this head-on with a **global survey approach** to understand how different cultures perceive and expect generative AI to represent them. The findings? Eye-opening, to say the least.

---

## 🌍 Why Culture Matters for Generative AI

Generative AI doesn't exist in a vacuum. It's trained on data that reflects certain worldviews, values, and communication styles. When you ask an AI to "write a polite email," what it considers polite might be straight out of a Midwestern American corporate handbook—not necessarily appropriate in Japan, Brazil, or Nigeria. Similarly, when generating stories, images, or recommendations, cultural context shapes what feels authentic vs. alienating.

The paper argues that **without empirical data from diverse regions**, we risk building AI systems that:
- Marginalize non-Western perspectives
- Perpetuate cultural stereotypes
- Fail to serve global users equitably
- Create friction in cross-cultural applications

---

## 🔬 How They Did It: The Global Survey

The researchers launched a comprehensive survey across **six continents**, covering:
- **North America** (US, Canada)
- **Europe** (UK, Germany, France, Spain)
- **East Asia** (China, Japan, South Korea)
- **Southeast Asia** (Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam)
- **South Asia** (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh)
- **Middle East & North Africa** (UAE, Egypt, Morocco)
- **Sub-Saharan Africa** (Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa)
- **Latin America** (Brazil, Mexico, Argentina)

Total respondents: **12,000+** adults, balanced for age, gender, and urban/rural distribution.

The survey measured:
- **Awareness** of generative AI (text, image, code, audio)
- **Usage patterns** (frequency, purposes, trust)
- **Cultural expectations** (how AI should represent local customs, languages, values)
- **Concerns** (bias, censorship, authenticity, job displacement)
- **Governance preferences** (who should regulate AI, what principles matter)

---

## 📊 Key Findings: A World of Divergence

### 1. **Trust in AI Varies Dramatically by Region**

- **Highest trust**: East Asia (China 62%, Japan 58%, South Korea 55%) — respondents generally believe AI can be a positive force if properly governed
- **Lowest trust**: Europe (UK 31%, Germany 28%, France 26%) — skeptical about AI's benefits, concerned about privacy and manipulation
- **Mixed**: North America (US 42%, Canada 38%) — optimistic but wary of misuse
- **Cautiously optimistic**: Latin America and Southeast Asia (50-55%) — see potential but want local control

### 2. **What "Cultural Representation" Means Differs Across Cultures**

When asked "How should AI represent your culture?" responses clustered:

- **East Asia**: Emphasize **social harmony** and **face-saving** — AI should avoid causing embarrassment, promote collective values
- **Europe**: Demand **transparency** and **individual sovereignty** — AI must disclose its training data, allow opt-out, respect GDPR-style rights
- **Southeast Asia**: Value **local languages** and **religious sensitivity** — AI should support regional dialects (e.g., Javanese, Isan) and avoid content offensive to Islamic/Buddhist/Hindu norms
- **South Asia**: Prioritize **caste and community awareness** — AI should understand social hierarchies and avoid inadvertently reinforcing discrimination
- **Latin America**: Focus on **inclusive representation** — AI should not stereotypically portray indigenous or Afro-Latinx communities
- **Middle East/North Africa**: Stress **religious authenticity** — depictions of prophets, sacred texts, and religious figures must adhere to scholarly interpretations
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: Want **decolonized AI** — move beyond Western-centric knowledge; incorporate oral traditions, local proverbs, indigenous knowledge systems

### 3. **Language Matters (But Not Just Translation)**

While everyone wants AI to support their native language, the survey revealed deeper expectations:
- **East Asia**: Prefer **honorifics and formality markers** encoded correctly (e.g., Korean speech levels, Japanese keigo)
- **Southeast Asia**: Need **code-switching support** (e.g., Taglish, Singlish) and **regional slang**
- **South Asia**: Expect **Hindi-Urdu script handling** (Devanagari vs. Perso-Arabic) and **colloquial variations**
- **Europe**: Demand **low-resource language parity** — Catalan, Basque, Breton should get same quality as French/German
- **Africa**: Insist on **oral-to-text transcription** capabilities for local languages without writing systems

### 4. **Governance: Who Should Control AI?**

There's no global consensus on who should set the rules:

- **EU-style regulation** favored in Europe and Canada (independent AI agencies, strict transparency)
- **Industry self-regulation** preferred in US and East Asia (fast innovation, light-touch oversight)
- **Hybrid models** popular in Southeast Asia and Latin America (government frameworks + industry codes)
- **Community-led governance** emerging in Africa and MENA (local councils, religious scholars, tribal elders)

Interestingly, **most regions rejected the idea of a single global AI regulator**, preferring context-specific approaches.

### 5. **The "Western Default" Problem is Real**

Across all non-Western regions, a common complaint: "AI assumes everyone is American/European." Examples cited:
- Default date formats (MM/DD/YYYY) ignoring local conventions
- Cultural references (Thanksgiving, Super Bowl, Eurovision) in examples
- Names that are predominantly Anglo in demos
- Holidays and weekends assumed to be Saturday-Sunday
- Measurement units (imperial vs. metric) baked into prompts

This isn't just annoying—it signals exclusion and makes AI feel "foreign" even when technically available.

## 💡 What This Means for AI Builders

If you're developing generative AI products, these findings are a wake-up call:

### Stop assuming "one size fits all"
- Don't export your cultural defaults; make them configurable
- Support local calendars, currencies, measurement units out of the box
- Handle names, addresses, and phone formats for every region you serve

### Invest in local cultural expertise
- Hire linguists and cultural anthropologists, not just engineers
- Partner with local universities and community organizations for guidance
- Conduct user research *in context*, not remote surveys

### Build flexibility into your models
- Allow users to specify cultural preferences (formality level, regional dialect, religious sensitivity)
- Avoid hard-coding assumptions about family structure, gender roles, or social hierarchies
- Make it easy to add new cultural variants without retraining entire models

### Advocate for inclusive governance
- Support multi-stakeholder AI oversight (government, civil society, technical experts)
- Push for transparency in training data provenance and cultural representation
- Fund research on low-resource languages and underrepresented cultures

## 🔮 The Road Ahead: Towards Culturally Intelligent AI

The authors propose a **Cultural Dimensions Framework** for AI evaluation, analogous to Hofstede's cultural dimensions but applied to AI behavior. Dimensions include:
- **Power distance** (how AI addresses authority figures)
- **Individualism vs. collectivism** (personal vs. group focus in recommendations)
- **Uncertainty avoidance** (how definitive vs. cautious AI should be)
- **Long-term orientation** (future-focused vs. present-focused advice)
- **Indulgence vs. restraint** (permissive vs. normative content generation)

Future work should test whether current LLMs can adapt to these dimensions through fine-tuning or prompting.

---

## Conclusion

This global survey shatters the myth that AI can be culturally neutral. Expectations for generative AI vary dramatically across regions, shaped by local values, histories, and social norms. The message is clear: **AI must become culturally intelligent, or it will fail to serve humanity's diversity**. That doesn't mean building a separate model for every culture—but it does mean designing systems flexible enough to respect cultural differences, and involving global communities in AI development from the start. The future of AI isn't just about scaling parameters; it's about scaling cultural competence.
