## Executive Summary

Amid an ongoing federal shutdown that has caused significant staffing shortages and security line delays at U.S. airports, the Trump administration has deployed U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents to over a dozen major airports. Eyewitness footage has captured at least one incident where ICE agents conducted arrests inside secure airport areas. This report examines the legal basis, operational implications, public reaction, and potential risks of using immigration enforcement personnel to supplement Transportation Security Administration (TSA) operations during a government shutdown.

## 1. Background: The Federal Shutdown and Airport Security Crisis

### 1.1 The Shutdown Context
- The federal government entered a shutdown in early March 2026 following Congressional failure to pass a continuing resolution[1].
- TSA, which operates under the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), employs approximately 47,000 Transportation Security Officers (TSOs) who are deemed essential but are working without pay[2].
- Historical data from previous shutdowns (2013, 2018–2019) show increased absenteeism among TSA workers as financial strain grows, leading to longer wait times and security backlogs[3].
- By mid-March 2026, average wait times at major hubs (e.g., Atlanta Hartsfield, Los Angeles LAX, New York JFK) exceeded 90 minutes, with some peaks above 2 hours[4].

### 1.2 TSA Staffing Shortages
- TSA typically operates at 90–95% staffing during normal operations. During the shutdown, absenteeism rose to an estimated 15–20% compared to baseline[5].
- The agency has attempted to mitigate by redeploying personnel from less busy airports, but coverage gaps remain.
- Union leaders for TSA employees have warned that prolonged unpaid work could lead to a wave of resignations, further exacerbating the crisis[6].

## 2. ICE Deployment: Scope and Authority

### 2.1 DHS Decision to Deploy ICE
On March 20, 2026, DHS Secretary announced that ICE agents would be temporarily assigned to assist with "security operations" at selected airports. The stated objectives were:
- **Augment screening personnel** at security checkpoints.
- **Conduct random document checks** for non-citizens in sterile areas (post-security).
- **Deter illegal entry attempts** through airports of entry[7].

### 2.2 Legal Authority
ICE’s primary mission is immigration enforcement within the interior of the United States. Its authority to operate in airports stems from:
- **INA § 287** (Immigration and Nationality Act): Grants ICE agents power to interrogate and arrest suspected immigration violators without a warrant if there is probable cause[8].
- **8 CFR § 287.5**: Specifies that ICE may conduct operations in "port-of-entry" areas, including sterile zones, but typically coordinates with CBP.
- **DHS Delegation**: The DHS Secretary can reallocate personnel across components (TSA, CBP, ICE) during emergencies[9].

Critics argue that using ICE for TSA duties may exceed statutory limits and blur the line between security screening and immigration enforcement[10].

## 4. Operational and Legal Implications

### 4.1 Impact on Airport Efficiency
- **Initial assessment**: The deployment has added approximately 200–300 ICE agents across 15 airports[17].
- **Effect on wait times**: Early data from TSA indicate a modest reduction in security lane delays (average wait down from 95 to 80 minutes in the first week), though this is within normal fluctuation[18].
- **Implementation challenges**: ICE agents are not trained in X-ray operation or passenger screening; they are primarily conducting document checks and responding to potential immigration violations, which does not directly speed up TSA lines[19].

### 4.2 Legal Concerns
- **Fourth Amendment**: Arrests in sterile areas without reasonable suspicion could be challenged as unreasonable seizures. Government claims that random document checks are permissible under immigration law, but courts have ruled such checks must not be discriminatory[20].
- **Supreme Court precedent**: In *United States v. Martinez-Fuerte* (1976), the Court allowed immigration checkpoints away from the border; however, airport interior checks are more intrusive[21].
- **Potential lawsuits**: Civil rights organizations are preparing class-action suits alleging racial profiling and violation of due process[22].

### 4.3 International and Diplomatic Fallout
- **Foreign governments** (Mexico, Canada, EU) have issued travel advisories urging their citizens to be aware of increased immigration enforcement at U.S. airports[23].
- **Aviation industry** groups (IATA, Airlines for America) are concerned about passenger experience and potential conflicts with international travelers[24].

## 5. Historical Context: ICE in Airports

ICE and its predecessor agencies (INS) have historically conducted immigration enforcement in airport terminals, but with important distinctions:
- **Prior practice**: ICE typically operated in "public areas" of airports (baggage claim, ticket counters) to intercept individuals who overstayed visas or entered fraudulently. They avoided sterile areas to not interfere with TSA operations[25].
- **Post-9/11 separation**: After 9/11, TSA was created and airport security zones were strictly separated from immigration enforcement to ensure security screening remained primary and unbiased[26].
- **Recent precedents**: In 2017, DHS considered using ICE to assist with airport security during a TSA strike threat, but the plan was shelved due to legal concerns[27]. The 2026 deployment marks the first large-scale, prolonged integration of ICE into security checkpoint operations.

### 6.1 Pros and Cons of the Deployment

|-----------------------|------------------------|

| May identify actual immigration violators who use air travel | Could trigger lawsuits and federal court injunctions, creating further operational uncertainty |

### 6.2 Alternative Approaches
- **Reallocate CBP officers** from less busy border sectors to airports (within statutory authority) – less controversial[30].
- **Accelerate TSA pay resolution**: Ensure TSA workers receive back pay promptly to reduce absenteeism[31].
- **Volunteer programs**: Recruit retired TSA officers or private security contractors with proper screening credentials[32].

## 7. Conclusion and Outlook

The deployment of ICE agents to U.S. airports during a federal shutdown represents a significant policy shift with complex implications. While it may provide short-term personnel augmentation, the move raises serious legal and societal concerns about the merging of security screening with immigration enforcement. The administration faces mounting pressure to resolve the shutdown and fully fund TSA to restore normal operations.

Key indicators to monitor:
- **Legal developments**: Any court orders restricting ICE activities in sterile areas.
- **TSA wait time trends**: Whether the deployment yields sustained improvement.
- **Public opinion**: Polling on airport safety and immigrant rights.
- **Congressional action**: Whether funding bills include provisions limiting ICE’s role in airports.

In the long term, sustainable airport security requires stable, well-compensated TSOs and clear boundaries between security and enforcement functions. The current experiment risk normalizing the use of interior enforcement assets in roles for which they are neither trained nor authorized, potentially eroding public trust in the aviation security system.
