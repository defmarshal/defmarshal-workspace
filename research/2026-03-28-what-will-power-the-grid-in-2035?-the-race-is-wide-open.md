## Executive Summary

The global power grid in 2035 stands at a crossroads. Multiple generation technologies—**advanced nuclear fission**, **nuclear fusion**, and **natural gas with carbon capture**—are positioned to deliver significant new capacity, competing alongside rapidly scaling **renewables plus storage** [1]. The race is exceptionally wide open because each technology faces distinct technical, economic, and regulatory hurdles, and no single solution has yet emerged as a clear winner. The outcome will determine whether the world meets its net-zero targets, maintains grid reliability, and controls energy costs. This report analyzes the contenders, their readiness timelines, and the factors that will decide which technologies power the grid in 2035.

## 1. Background: The 2035 Grid Challenge

### 1.1. Why 2035 Matters
- **Policy deadlines**: Many countries (EU, UK, Canada, Japan) have legally binding **net-zero electricity sector targets between 2035 and 2040** [2]
- **Demand explosion**: Electrification of transport, heating, and industry could increase electricity demand by **30–50%** by 2035 [3]
- **Intermittency problem**: Solar and wind already constitute >30% of new capacity in many regions; without long-duration storage or firm low-carbon power, grid stability becomes challenging
- **Aging infrastructure**: Existing coal and nuclear plants are retiring; replacements must be ready by 2035

The 2035 grid must be **low-carbon, dispatchable, scalable, and cost-effective**. No current technology fully satisfies all four.

## 2. The Contenders: Technologies in the Race

### 2.1. Advanced Nuclear Fission (Generation IV & SMRs)

**Technology status**:
- **Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)**: NuScale, GE-Hitachi BWRX-300, Rolls-Royce Compact
- **Generation IV concepts**: Molten salt, high-temperature gas-cooled, sodium-cooled fast reactors
- **Latest timeline**: First commercial SMRs (NuScale) targeted for **2029–2030** in the US; SMRs in China (ACP100) already connected to grid (2023) [4]

**Pros**:
- **Highly reliable** (90%+ capacity factor)
- **Low-carbon** (life-cycle emissions ~12 gCO₂/kWh)
- **Compact footprint** suitable for near-term replacement of retiring plants
- **Regulatory progress**: US NRC design approvals for SMRs in 2024–2025

**Cons**:
- **High capital cost** ($6,000–$9,000/kW), though SMRs promise factory-built cost reductions
- **Waste disposal** remains politically contentious
- **Construction timelines** often slip (Vogtle expansion delays)
- **Public acceptance** post-Fukushima still a barrier in some regions

**Projected 2035 contribution**: Could deliver **50–100 GW** globally if SMR deployments accelerate [5].

---

### 2.2. Nuclear Fusion

**Technology status**:
- **Magnetic confinement (tokamak)**: ITER (France) aims for first plasma 2025, deuterium-tritium operation 2035; DEMO (successor) targeted for 2050 [6]
- **Inertial confinement**: NIF achieves ignition (2022), but not yet net electricity gain
- **Private fusion startups**: Commonwealth Fusion Systems (SPARC), Helion Energy, TAE Technologies, Zap Energy pursuing faster timelines—some aim for grid connection by **early 2030s**

**Pros**:
- **Abundant fuel**: Deuterium from seawater, lithium for tritium breeding
- **Minimal long-lived waste**
- **Inherently safe** (no meltdown risk)
- **Potential for lower cost** at scale if physics challenges solved

**Cons**:
- **Extreme technical difficulty**: Sustained net electricity gain not yet demonstrated
- **Materials science**: Neutron damage to reactor walls remains unsolved
- **Regulatory framework** for fusion power nonexistent
- **Private startup risk**: Many may fail before demonstration

**Projected 2035 contribution**: Optimistic scenario: **5–20 GW** from private fusion pilots; most likely **<5 GW** or zero. Fusion unlikely to be a major grid contributor by 2035 [7].

---

### 2.3. Natural Gas with Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)

**Technology status**:
- ** turbine + post-combustion amine scrubbing**: Mature but expensive; Petra Nova (US) and Boundary Dam (Canada) operating at small scale
- **Allam-cycle** natural gas turbines (NetPower): 50 MW demonstration operational; larger plants planned
- **Cost**: CCS adds ~$50–$100/MWh to gas generation; needs carbon price > $80/ton to be viable

**Pros**:
- **Dispatchable** (quick ramp, high capacity factor)
- **Uses existing gas infrastructure** and fuel supply chains
- **Lower capital cost** than nuclear
- **Bridge role**: Can replace coal while renewables scale

**Cons**:
- **Carbon capture efficiency** typically 85–95%, not 100%
- **Storage verification**: Long-term geological storage monitoring required
- **Methane leakage** upstream erodes climate benefit
- **Public opposition** to CO₂ pipelines and storage sites

**Projected 2035 contribution**: With strong carbon pricing, **20–50 GW** of gas+CCS could be built, mostly in US, China, Europe [8].

---

### 2.4. Renewables Plus Storage (Wind, Solar, Batteries)

**Technology status**:
- **Solar PV**: $0.03–0.05/kWh utility-scale; efficiency ~22%
- **Onshore/offshore wind**: $0.03–0.06/kWh
- **Lithium-ion batteries**: $150–200/kWh; 4–8 hour duration standard; long-duration (10–100h) emerging (e.g., Form Energy iron-air)
- **Grid-scale storage deployments**: Growing exponentially (US > 30 GWh installed 2025)

**Pros**:
- **Lowest cost** new generation in most markets [9]
- **Rapid deployment**: Solar farms in <1 year
- **Distributed potential**: Reduces transmission needs
- **Public acceptance** generally high

**Cons**:
- **Intermittency** requires backup or storage for >50% penetration
- **Long-duration storage** (>10h) not yet cost-competitive
- **Material supply** challenges (lithium, cobalt, rare earths)
- **Land use** constraints in densely populated regions

**Projected 2035 contribution**: Dominant new capacity; could supply **40–60%** of electricity in many regions, but actual contribution limited by storage availability [10].

---

### 2.5. Other Emerging Technologies

- **Geothermal (enhanced)**: EGS drilling costs dropping; could provide baseload; **5–10 GW** possible by 2035
- **Tidal/wave**: Still high cost; unlikely >1 GW by 2035
- **Hydrogen (blue/green)**: More likely for industry/transport than bulk power generation; fuel cells too expensive for baseload
- **Biomass with CCS**: Limited by sustainable feedstock; potential 5 GW

## 4. Regional Variations in 2035 Mix

### 4.1. United States
- **Policy**: Inflation Reduction Act incentives extend through 2032; may need renewal
- **Likely mix**: 40% renewables, 20% nuclear (existing + SMRs), 15% gas+CCS, 25% storage/other

## 5. Wild Cards That Could Shift the Race

### 5.1. Breakthrough in Long-Duration Storage
- If flow batteries, compressed air, or novel chemistries drop below $50/kWh and achieve 100+ hour duration, **renewables could supply >80%** of grid without nuclear/fossil backup
- Companies to watch: Form Energy, ESS Inc., Highview Power

### 5.2. Fusion Surprise
- A private startup (e.g., Helion, Commonwealth) achieves **net electricity gain and sustained operation** before 2030, triggering massive investment
- This could fast-track fusion pilots to 2032–2035, adding 10+ GW

### 5.3. Carbon Pricing Implementation
- Global carbon price > $100/ton would make **gas+CCS and nuclear instantly competitive**
- Current average carbon price: ~$6/ton; only EU ~$80, California ~$50

### 5.4. Geopolitical Supply Chain Disruptions
- Rare earth shortages could slow wind turbine deployments
- Uranium supply constraints (Kazakhstan, Canada) could limit nuclear expansion
- Semiconductor shortages affect power electronics for renewables/inverters

### 5.5. Public Opinion Shifts
- Another nuclear accident (even minor) could halt SMR deployments
- Climate-driven urgency could override local opposition to transmission/storage projects

## 6. Conclusion: No Silver Bullet, But a Mix

The 2035 grid will **not** be powered by a single technology. The "race" is actually a **portfolio competition**. The most likely outcome is:

- **Renewables + storage** become the **largest single source** (40–50% of capacity, but lower share of actual generation due to intermittency)
- **Nuclear fission (SMRs + Gen III+)** provides **firm low-carbon baseload** (15–25% share), especially in countries with existing nuclear infrastructure
- **Natural gas with CCS** fills **mid-merit gaps** and provides grid flexibility (10–15%), contingent on carbon pricing
- **Fusion** contributes minimally (<1%) by 2035, mostly pilot projects
- **Hydro, geothermal, biomass** fill niche roles

The decisive factor will be **policy**: carbon pricing, streamlined permitting for transmission and nuclear, and R&D support for long-duration storage. Without aggressive policy, coal may linger, and decarbonization will slip behind 2035 targets.

The race remains wide open because **each technology's success depends on variables outside pure engineering**—regulatory speed, public acceptance, investor patience, and geopolitical stability. The next decade will test whether the world can build an energy system that is simultaneously **clean, reliable, and affordable**.
